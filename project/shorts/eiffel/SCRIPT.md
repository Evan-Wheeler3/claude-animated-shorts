# "The Eiffel Tower's Secret Job" — script v2 (approved 2026-11-13)
Format: ~58 s vertical short, one steady narrator, no captions.

Facts (checked):
- Built for the 1889 World's Fair on a 20-year permit expiring in 1909; designed to be dismantled (History.com, toureiffel.paris).
- 14 Feb 1887: protest letter in Le Temps by artists and writers (Maupassant, Dumas fils...), "useless and monstrous".
- Maupassant is said to have eaten in the tower's restaurant because it was the one place he couldn't see it (secondhand: "supposedly").
- Eiffel financed wireless telegraphy experiments from 1898; antenna at the summit (1903, Ferrie). Its military radio
  value got the concession renewed from 1 Jan 1910 for 70 years (toureiffel.paris).
- Tallest man-made structure on Earth 1889-1930.
- Time signals for ships from 23 May 1910, at the request of the Bureau des Longitudes, to find longitude at sea
  (SAF; NIST; 1910 newspapers).
- WWI: intercepted enemy radio (History.com).
- Today: ~30 DTT channels and 32 radio stations; a DAB+ antenna in 2022 took it from 324 to 330 m (SETE press release, WorldDAB).
- The last line is a joke: it broadcasts TV/radio, not Wi-Fi.

The Eiffel Tower has a secret job. And it's the only reason it's still standing.
In 1889, Paris built it for the World's Fair, with a permit for just twenty years. After that, it was coming down.
And honestly, Paris couldn't wait. Famous artists signed a letter calling it useless and monstrous.
One writer supposedly ate lunch inside it every day, because it was the only place in Paris where he couldn't see it.
Gustave Eiffel had until 1909 to make his tower useful. So he found it a job. Radio.
You see, a radio signal spreads out from its antenna, and anything in the way, hills, buildings, even the curve of the Earth, blocks it.
So the higher the antenna, the farther it reaches. Ever noticed your Wi-Fi works better upstairs than in the basement? Same idea.
And back then, the Eiffel Tower was the tallest structure on Earth. Basically, the world's biggest antenna.
The army loved it, and Paris renewed the permit for seventy more years.
From the top, it sent time signals to ships at sea, so they could work out where they were. And in the war, it listened in on enemy radio.
It's still covered in antennas today. In 2022, a new one made it six metres taller.
So if you ever propose at the top of the Eiffel Tower, technically, you're kneeling on a giant Wi-Fi router.
