# "The Tower Paris Wanted Gone" (Eiffel Tower) — script v1 for approval (2026-11-13)
Format: ~48 s vertical short, one steady narrator.

Facts (checked):
- Built for the 1889 World's Fair on a 20-year permit expiring in 1909; designed to be easy to dismantle (History.com, toureiffel.paris).
- 14 Feb 1887: a protest letter in Le Temps signed by artists and writers incl. Guy de Maupassant and Alexandre Dumas fils;
  it called the tower "useless and monstrous" (toureiffel.paris, Wikisource, JSTOR Daily).
- Maupassant is said to have eaten regularly in the tower's restaurant because it was the one place in Paris he
  couldn't see it (widely repeated, secondhand: hence "supposedly").
- Eiffel made the tower a laboratory (weather, physics) and financed wireless telegraphy experiments from 1898; an
  antenna on the summit for Gustave Ferrie in 1903 (toureiffel.paris, History.com).
- Its military radio value led Paris to renew the concession from 1 Jan 1910, for 70 years (toureiffel.paris).
- WWI: the tower's station intercepted enemy radio messages; widely credited with help before the Marne (1914) and
  with intercepts used against Mata Hari (History.com; secondary sources: "helped").
- Tallest man-made structure in the world from 1889 to 1930.

The Eiffel Tower was supposed to be torn down.
It was built for the World's Fair in 1889, with a permit for just twenty years.
And Paris hated it. Famous artists signed a letter calling it useless and monstrous.
One writer supposedly ate lunch inside it every day, because it was the only place in Paris where he couldn't see it.
Gustave Eiffel had until 1909 to make his tower useful.
So he turned it into a giant science experiment. Weather instruments, physics tests, and finally, a radio antenna on the very top.
That did it. The army realised the tallest structure on Earth could send messages farther than anything else.
Paris renewed his permit for another seventy years.
Then the war came. The tower nobody wanted listened in on enemy radio, and helped catch one of the most famous spies in history.
