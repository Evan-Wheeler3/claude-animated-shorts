# "A Whale of a Deal" — original script v1 (2026-11-12)
Format: ~68 s vertical short, ~3.7 words/s, one steady narrator, no captions.

Facts (checked against several sources; see links):
- 12 Nov 1970, beach near Florence, Oregon: a 45 ft, ~8 ton sperm whale had washed ashore about three days earlier and stank.
- Oregon's ocean beaches were legally public highways (1913 law), so the State Highway Division had the job. Engineer George
  Thornton's team decided to remove it the way they would a boulder: dynamite, hoping to blow it to bits small enough for gulls and crabs.
- 20 cases (about half a ton) of dynamite were used. Walter Umenhofer, a WWII veteran with explosives training who was in the
  crowd, warned that 20 sticks would have been enough; he was ignored.
- Spectators and the KATU news crew (reporter Paul Linnman) stood about a quarter mile away. The blast threw sand and blubber
  ~100 ft up; chunks rained on the crowd; nobody was hurt. One slab (reported ~3 ft across) crushed the roof of a parked car.
- Most of the carcass stayed where it was; the gulls had been scared off; highway crews buried the remains.
- The crushed car was Umenhofer's new Oldsmobile, bought at Dunham Oldsmobile in Eugene during a "Get a Whale of a Deal"
  promotion. The state reimbursed him.

In 1970, Oregon tried to get rid of a dead whale with half a ton of dynamite.
An eight-ton sperm whale washes up near the town of Florence, and after three days, the smell is unbearable.
Back then, Oregon's beaches legally counted as highways, so the job goes to the highway department.
And to a highway engineer, a dead whale is just a really big boulder.
The plan is simple. Blow it into tiny pieces, and let the seagulls eat the rest.
So they pack twenty cases of dynamite under the whale.
A man in the crowd, trained in explosives during the war, says twenty sticks would do it. Nobody listens.
Everyone backs up a quarter of a mile, a news crew starts rolling, and the engineer pushes the plunger.
The whale goes up in a hundred-foot cloud of sand and blubber. And then it starts coming down.
Luckily, everyone was standing a quarter of a mile away.
Unluckily, that's exactly where the whale landed.
Chunks rain down on the crowd, and one slab three feet wide flattens the roof of a parked car.
You see, dynamite doesn't make things disappear. It just moves them, very fast, in every direction.
Ever stomped on a ketchup packet? The ketchup isn't gone. It's just on everybody now.
Most of the whale never even moved, and the seagulls were long gone.
As for the flattened car, it belonged to the man who warned them.
He'd just bought it at a sale called: Get a Whale of a Deal.
