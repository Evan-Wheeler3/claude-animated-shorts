# "Why Plane Windows Are Round" — script v2 (approved 2026-11-13)
Format: ~55 s vertical short, one steady narrator, no captions.

Facts (checked):
- The de Havilland Comet (in service 1952) was the world's first jet airliner. Two broke up in flight in 1954, within
  about four months (BOAC 781, January, off Elba; South African Airways 201, April, off Naples). The fleet was grounded.
- At RAE Farnborough a complete fuselage was submerged in a water tank and pressurised repeatedly to simulate flights;
  it failed by fatigue cracking at a window cutout corner. Sources give different cycle counts, so the script says
  "thousands of flights" (Engineers Ireland, MigFlug, AeroXplorer).
- Cabins are pressurised at cruise altitude; each flight is one pressurisation cycle. Sharp corners concentrate stress;
  rounded openings spread it. Later jets (incl. the Comet 4) have rounded windows.
- The small "breather" hole in the middle pane equalises pressure so the outer pane carries the load (and limits fogging).

In 1954, the most advanced airliner in the world kept breaking apart in the sky. And the problem was the windows.
The de Havilland Comet was the world's first passenger jet. Fast, quiet, and full of big, square windows.
Then, within four months, two of them broke apart in mid-air.
So engineers did something wild. They sank an entire plane in a giant water tank, and pumped it full of pressure, over and over, like thousands of flights in a row.
Eventually, the metal split open. Right at the corner of a window.
But why the corner?
Up where jets fly, the air is too thin to breathe. So the cabin gets pumped full of air, and the whole plane swells up a tiny bit, like a balloon. Then it shrinks back down when you land.
All that pushing has to flow around every hole in the plane, the way water flows around a rock.
A round window lets it flow smoothly around the edge. A square window forces it all into the sharp corners.
And it happens every single flight. Ever bent a paperclip back and forth? It doesn't break the first time. But eventually, it snaps right at the bend.
That's why every plane window today is round.
And that tiny hole in your window? It's supposed to be there. So please stop calling the flight attendant.
