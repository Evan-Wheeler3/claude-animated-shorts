# "The Trojan Horse" — script v1 (approved 2026-11-13)
Format: ~55 s vertical short, one steady narrator, no captions.

Facts (checked):
- The Iliad ends before the wooden horse (Hector's funeral); the Odyssey mentions it in passing (Demodocus, Odysseus);
  the fullest account is Virgil's Aeneid, Book 2 (Roman, 1st c. BC), incl. Laocoon's warning and the sea serpents.
- Theories (hedged in the script): a siege engine covered in damp horse hides; an earthquake (Poseidon, god of horses
  and earthquakes; Troy VI shows earthquake damage, though this is disputed as the cause of the war layer).
- Troy = Hisarlik, Turkey. Heinrich Schliemann (a businessman) dug his Great Trench 1871-1873 down to bedrock,
  destroying much of Troy VI and VII (the layers linked to the war) and mistaking Troy II for Priam's city.
Sources: en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schliemann's_Trench · worldhistory.org/article/2196 · history.com (Trojan horse gullibility) ·
historyskills.com (Trojan War fact or fiction) · nationalgeographic.com (the lost city of Troy) · worldatlas.com

The Trojan Horse is the greatest trick in history. There's just one problem. It's not in the Iliad.
For ten years, the Greeks attack the city of Troy, and get nowhere.
So they build a giant wooden horse, hide soldiers inside, and sail away, pretending to give up.
The Trojans drag the horse inside their walls to celebrate.
One priest warns them: beware of Greeks bearing gifts. Two giant sea serpents rise out of the water and crush him.
That night, the soldiers climb out, open the gates, and Troy falls.
Except Homer's Iliad ends before any of this. Homer barely mentions the horse. The famous version comes from a Roman poet, more than a thousand years after the war.
So what was the horse, really?
You see, stories passed down for centuries tend to grow. Ever noticed your uncle's fishing story gets bigger every time he tells it?
Some historians think the "horse" was a battering ram covered in wet horse hides. Others think it was an earthquake, because Poseidon was the god of horses and earthquakes, and one layer of Troy really was shaken apart.
Troy itself was real. In the 1870s, a businessman named Heinrich Schliemann found it in Turkey.
But he dug a giant trench straight through the hill, looking for the famous city, and smashed right through the layers from the time of the actual war.
So the Greeks spent ten years trying to destroy Troy. Schliemann did it in three.
