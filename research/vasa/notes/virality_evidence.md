# Virality drivers for a 30-45 s animated true-history Short (evidence-graded, 2010-2026)

Grading key: **strong** = large dataset or randomized/meta-analytic evidence, peer reviewed; **moderate** = peer reviewed but observational, a single context, or small; **weak** = vendor/industry analysis with undisclosed method, or secondary summaries only; **anecdote** = creator lore or commentary.
Transfer flag: **[T!]** = the finding comes from text/news articles, Twitter/Facebook, or long-form video and may not transfer to 30-45 s vertical video.
Method note: these come from about 15 search and fetch calls. Fetches of techcrunch.com and pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov failed (those hosts are outside this session's network allowlist), so a few items rest on search-result abstracts or summaries. Those items are marked "(abstract/summary only)".

## 1. Academic research on sharing: arousal, awe, anger, practical value, STEPPS, social currency, surprise, humour

### Takeaway
The best-supported academic finding is that **high-arousal emotion (awe, anger, anxiety) predicts sharing and low-arousal sadness predicts less sharing**. That comes from one large observational study of NYT articles (2012). Later large datasets show negativity, moral-emotional language and out-group framing raising engagement on Twitter, Facebook and headline tests. All of it comes from text or news, not short video **[T!]**. STEPPS is a popular synthesis, not a validated model.

### Cited Findings
- Berger & Milkman (2012), *Journal of Marketing Research*, "What Makes Online Content Viral?". They analysed every New York Times article over a ~3-month window against the most-emailed list. Content evoking **high-arousal positive (awe) or negative (anger, anxiety)** emotion was more viral, and low-arousal **sadness** was associated with less sharing. The authors report the effect held when controlling for how surprising, interesting or practically useful the content was. Grade: **moderate-to-strong** (large, but observational, a single outlet, email sharing) **[T!]** — [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf); [Northeastern copy](https://cssh.northeastern.edu/pandemic-teaching-initiative/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2020/09/What-Makes-Online-Content-Viral.pdf)
- Berger himself said in a 2023 interview that he wished they had run an experiment rather than relying on scraped observational data. He also says the same patterns were largely predictive of which **summaries of scientific findings** get shared. The paper won Sage's 10-Year Impact Award. Grade: **anecdote** (author's own remarks) — [Social Science Space, 2023](https://www.socialsciencespace.com/2023/05/a-viral-paper-on-determining-what-makes-online-content-viral/); [Penn CHIBE repost](https://chibe.upenn.edu/news/social-science-space-a-viral-paper-on-determining-what-makes-online-content-viral/)
- I found no published direct replication or formal critique of Berger & Milkman in this search. — (search returned none; see Gaps)
- Brady et al. (2017), *PNAS*. They analysed ~563,000 tweets on gun control, same-sex marriage and climate change. Each additional **moral-emotional word** raised diffusion by ~20%, and the effect was stronger within ideological networks than between them. Grade: **moderate** (large, but observational, Twitter, political topics) **[T!]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28652356/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5514704)
- Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden (2021), *PNAS*. They analysed 2,730,215 posts from news-media and US congressional accounts on Facebook and Twitter. Posts about the political **out-group** were shared roughly twice as often. Out-group language was ~4.8x as strong a predictor as negative-affect words and ~6.7x as strong as moral-emotional words, and it drew more "angry" reactions. Secondary summaries give conflicting per-word effect sizes (shares versus odds). Grade: **moderate** (large, observational, political) **[T!]** — [ADS abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PNAS..11824292R/abstract); [Cambridge summary](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/viralpolitics)
- Robertson et al. (2023), *Nature Human Behaviour*, "Negativity drives online news consumption". They used Upworthy's randomized headline A/B tests (~105,000 variants, ~5.7M clicks, >370M impressions, 2012-2015). Negative words raised CTR by ~2.3% per word for an average-length headline, and positive words lowered it. Nieman Lab reports **sad** words beat angry or scary ones for clicks. That is a contrast with Berger & Milkman, but this study measures *clicks*, not *shares*. Grade: **strong** for headline clicks (randomized, within-story), but an era- and platform-specific measure **[T!]** — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01538-4); [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/03/negative-words-in-news-headlines-generate-more-clicks-but-sad-words-are-more-effective-than-angry-or-scary-ones/)
- Berger's STEPPS (*Contagious*, 2013) has six drivers: **Social currency** (people share what makes them look smart or in-the-know), **Triggers** (environmental cues keep an idea top of mind), **Emotion** (high-arousal), **Public** (observable behaviour gets imitated), **Practical value** (useful information gets passed on) and **Stories** ("Trojan horse" narratives that carry the idea). Grade: **weak as a model** (a popular synthesis of Berger's research and others', not validated as a framework; individual pillars draw on separate studies) — [Stanford GSB, 2013](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/why-do-some-ideas-spread); [Penn Today Q&A, 2013](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/2013-04-18/interviews/qa-jonah-berger); [APA podcast](https://www.apa.org/news/podcasts/speaking-of-psychology/viral)

### Inferences
- For a Vasa Short, the arousal finding points to **awe** (the scale and ornament of the ship, its salvage after 333 years) and mild **outrage or incredulity** (known design flaws, the failed stability test before launch) as the emotional levers. A sad or tragic tone alone (deaths) is the low-arousal register Berger & Milkman associated with less sharing. This is inference from text-article data.
- "Social currency" maps onto a fact a viewer can repeat to look clever ("it sank 1,300 m into its maiden voyage, in harbour"). That is plausible but untested for Shorts.
- Out-group and moral-outrage effects come from political content. A history Short can borrow mild "blame" framing (the king's demands, the shipwright's choices) without partisan out-group framing, but nothing in the evidence tests that.

### Gaps
- No direct replication or critique of Berger & Milkman turned up. A targeted Google Scholar search is still needed.
- I did not retrieve primary sources for experimental work on **awe**, **surprise** or **humour** causing sharing, such as Berger (2011, *Psychological Science*, physiological arousal and sharing). I know of it from prior literature but did not verify it this session, so it is not cited as a finding.
- No study tested these emotion-sharing effects on short vertical video.

## 2. Curiosity gap / information-gap theory, and whether clickbait hurts

### Takeaway
Loewenstein's (1994) information-gap theory is well established: curiosity arises from noticing a manageable gap in knowledge. Direct evidence that clickbait-style gaps raise engagement is **mixed**, and there is some evidence of a **credibility cost**. I found no evidence on short-video retention specifically.

### Cited Findings
- Loewenstein (1994) defines curiosity as attention focused on a gap in one's knowledge, which produces a feeling of deprivation. It requires some prior knowledge, and small, closable gaps produce more curiosity than huge ones. Grade: **strong as theory** (highly cited, with experimental follow-ups) — [Golman & Loewenstein, "Information Gaps" (CMU PDF)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/Information%20Gaps--Golman%20and%20Loewenstein.pdf); [Golman & Loewenstein on curiosity (CMU PDF)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)
- A three-study clickbait paper (CHI 2021, Penn State; authors not confirmed this session) found that clickbait headlines **did not reliably produce more curiosity or more clicks** than non-clickbait in two studies. Automated clickbait detectors agreed with each other only 47% of the time. Grade: **moderate** (experiments, news headlines) **[T!]** (abstract/summary only) — [Penn State PDF](https://pike.psu.edu/publications/chi21.pdf); [Pure record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/does-clickbait-actually-atract-more-clicks-three-clickbait-studie/)
- An MTurk study (n≈200) found that clickbait headlines **significantly reduced the credibility** of a news item with content held constant, though not of the outlet (Hindi news). Grade: **weak-moderate** (small, a single context) **[T!]** (summary only) — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/168209/1/Clickbaits_PURE.PDF)
- In a PLOS ONE analysis of Facebook posts, some clickbait features (unusual punctuation, stock phrases) raised interaction and others lowered it. Grade: **moderate** (observational) **[T!]** (summary only) — [PMC8799425](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8799425)

### Inferences
- A curiosity gap that **resolves inside the video** ("Why did the most powerful warship in the world sink in 20 minutes?", answered by 35 s) fits the theory and avoids the bait-and-switch credibility cost. A hook that promises more than the video delivers is where the evidence (weakly) points to harm.
- The small-gap principle suggests anchoring the hook in something viewers half-know (a ship sinking) rather than an obscure term ("the Vasa stability test").

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring whether unresolved curiosity on short-form video lowers satisfaction, completion or follows. TikTok and YouTube do not publish this.
- I did not verify neuroscience evidence that curiosity improves memory, such as Kang et al. 2009 (*Psychological Science*) or Gruber et al. 2014 (*Neuron*). It is known from prior literature but not cited here.

## 3. Storytelling structure and narrative transportation; story versus fact-list formats

### Takeaway
A meta-analysis supports **narrative transportation** (being absorbed in a story) shifting beliefs and attitudes. A separate meta-analysis tests whether narratives are **better remembered and understood** than expository text and suggests an advantage, but I could not retrieve its effect sizes. Neither tests 30-45 s video.

### Cited Findings
- van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti & Wetzels (2014), *Journal of Consumer Research* 40:797-817, is a meta-analysis of **132 effect sizes from 76 articles** on narrative transportation. It proposes an extended transportation-imagery model in which transportation drives changes in affective and cognitive responses, beliefs, attitudes and intentions. Grade: **strong** (meta-analysis; mostly text, ads and film) **[T!]** — [City Research Online PDF](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/1/SSRN-id2033192.pdf)
- Green & Brock (2000) introduced transportation theory: entering the story-world reduces counter-arguing and shifts beliefs. Grade: **strong as a foundational construct** — [Wikipedia overview (secondary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_theory_(psychology))
- A meta-analysis of experiments comparing memory and comprehension for **narrative versus expository** texts (authors and journal not confirmed this session) is motivated by theories predicting a narrative advantage, and earlier results were mixed. I could not retrieve the overall effect, sample or date this session, so treat the direction ("narratives somewhat better recalled and comprehended") as **unverified**. **[T!]** (text, not video) — [PMC8219577](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577)

### Inferences
- A Vasa Short structured as a tight story (a protagonist such as King Gustav II Adolf or the shipwright, rising stakes, a reversal, a payoff) is better aligned with the evidence than a "5 facts about the Vasa" list. The evidence comes from longer texts, though, and 30-45 s may be too short for deep transportation.

### Gaps
- No study compares story versus list formats in **sub-60 s video** for sharing or retention.
- The exact effect sizes for the narrative-versus-expository meta-analysis need checking (blocked host).

## 4. What drives comments specifically; risks of deliberate errors and rage-bait

### Takeaway
Evidence on comment drivers is **thin**. Large studies link moral, outrage and out-group content to more engagement (including angry reactions), and controversy and toxicity correlate with comment engagement on YouTube, at a monetization cost. I found **no rigorous evidence** that planting deliberate errors ("Cunningham's Law" / "well actually" bait) helps. It is creator lore, with credibility risk.

### Cited Findings
- Rathje et al. (2021): out-group posts drew more "angry" reactions and more shares. Grade: **moderate** **[T!]** — [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2021PNAS..11824292R/abstract)
- "The Monetisation of Toxicity" (2024, arXiv preprint) studied 20 controversial YouTube channels: **toxic comments correlate with higher engagement but hurt monetisation**. Grade: **weak-moderate** (preprint, a small channel sample, long-form) **[T!]** (abstract only) — [arXiv 2408.00534](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00534v1)
- Comment counts are a rough proxy for controversy, and coordinated "commenter mobs" can inflate them. Grade: **weak-moderate** (preprints) — [arXiv 2304.07681](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07681); [arXiv 1907.00435](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.00435)
- The clickbait credibility penalty (section 2) is the nearest evidence on the cost of misleading viewers. Grade: **weak-moderate** — [White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/168209/1/Clickbaits_PURE.PDF)
- Public-history commentary warns that unsourced, counterintuitive "experts are wrong" history content performs well and spreads misinformation. Grade: **anecdote / commentary** — [The Walrus](https://thewalrus.ca/?p=162376); [Medium, public historian](https://medium.com/@alexandriaelyssep/responsibility-and-the-public-historian-how-historians-can-use-short-form-media-to-combat-3b5fbd643fd6)

### Inferences
- Legitimate comment prompts for Vasa include genuinely debatable questions where historians do disagree or responsibility is contested (who was to blame: the king, the shipwright Henrik Hybertsson who died before launch, or the admiral who halted the stability test?). That invites "well actually" engagement without lying.
- Deliberate factual errors carry reputational risk for an educational brand, and nothing found shows a net benefit.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study on what drives comment *volume* on Shorts or TikTok, nor on question-prompt CTAs.
- The "Cunningham's Law" deliberate-error tactic has no empirical source.

## 5. Rewatch and loop behaviour on short-form platforms

### Takeaway
Since 31 March 2025, YouTube counts **every start and replay** of a Short as a view. Loops therefore inflate public view counts, while "engaged views" (used for monetization) are separate. Peer-reviewed work finds **video duration is the strongest predictor of watching to the end** on TikTok. I found **no peer-reviewed evidence** that loops causally boost distribution. That is mostly lore.

### Cited Findings
- YouTube changed Shorts view counting on 31 Mar 2025: a view is counted each time a Short starts or **replays**, with no minimum watch time, aligning with TikTok and Reels. The old metric became "engaged views" (Advanced Mode), and YPP eligibility and revenue stay tied to engaged views. Grade: **strong for the fact** (official change, reported by multiple outlets; I did not reach the YouTube primary page) — [TechCrunch 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/26/youtube-is-changing-how-youtube-shorts-views-are-counted); [9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2025/03/26/youtube-shorts-views-metric/); [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-changes-shorts-view-counts-no-change-to-monetization/543005/)
- A third-party blog quotes YouTube Help as defining engaged views as watching "past the initial seconds, not including any loops". Unverified. Grade: **weak** — [mubert.com blog](https://mubert.com/blog/?p=5501)
- Coimbra Vieira, Zannettou et al. (Max Planck): in controlled and real-world TikTok data, **video duration is the strongest predictor** of whether a video is watched to the end, and demographics add little. Grade: **moderate** (abstract only) — [MPIDR record](https://www.demogr.mpg.de/en/publications_databases_6118/publications_1904/journal_articles/exploring_the_limits_of_predicting_user_watching_behavior_with_short_form_videos_on_tiktok_8988/)
- Salminen, Jansen et al. (HT '24) looked at TikTok ads from one e-commerce advertiser (mean length 56.7 s). **Most viewers leave within the first quarter.** Grade: **moderate-weak** (a single advertiser, ads) — [Univ. Vaasa repository](https://osuva.uwasa.fi/bitstreams/c544c632-31e2-4fed-80bd-f6044705ce51/download)
- Vendor "benchmark" completion rates (e.g. ~89% for 7 s clips versus ~8% for 10-minute videos) are self-described estimates, not a study. Grade: **weak** — [ttcalculator.net](https://ttcalculator.net/learn/completion-rate-by-video-length/)
- TikTok's "rewatched" metric does not distinguish enjoyment from confusion. Grade: **anecdote** — [Lilach Bullock blog](https://www.lilachbullock.com/what-does-rewatched-mean-on-tiktok-analytics/)

### Inferences
- A seamless end-to-start loop (the last frame flows into the hook) raises the public view count on YouTube after 2025, but not engaged views. Whether it raises *recommendation* is not publicly documented.
- Duration predicting completion argues for staying near 30-35 s rather than 45 s unless the story needs the time.

### Gaps
- There is no official YouTube or TikTok statement that rewatches are weighted in ranking (TikTok says only "watch time" and "whether you finish a video" in general terms; I could not fetch the newsroom page in full).
- No peer-reviewed study isolates the causal effect of looping on reach.

## 6. Humour in educational content: comprehension and engagement

### Takeaway
Four decades of classroom research (Banas et al. 2011) show humour reliably improves **perceptions of the instructor and climate**. Effects on **learning are mixed**, and they are best when the humour is related to the content and non-hostile. That is classroom evidence, not short video **[T!]**.

### Cited Findings
- Banas, Dunbar, Rodriguez & Liu (2011), "A review of humor in education settings: Four decades of research", *Communication Education* 60(1):115-144. Humour is linked to positive perceptions of the instructor and learning environment. The learning evidence is "considerably more mixed", and the review warns against hostile or disparaging humour. Grade: **moderate** (narrative review, classrooms) (secondary summaries only) — [Faculty Focus summary](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/effective-teaching-strategies/humor-in-the-classroom-40-years-of-research/); [NCA Communication Currents](https://natcom.org/node/732)
- A 2025 review of five decades of instructional-humour research exists. I did not retrieve its findings. — [PMC11814177](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11814177/)
- Practitioner summaries say humour works best sandwiched between an explanation and a repetition of the concept. Grade: **weak** (secondary) — [CU Boulder teaching resource](https://www.colorado.edu/center/teaching-learning/teaching-resources/student-engagement/learner-motivation/enhancing-meaning)

### Inferences
- Content-related humour (the absurdity of a ship sinking in sight of the launch crowd, or the stability "test" of 30 sailors running side to side being stopped) is consistent with the evidence. Gags unrelated to the content risk distracting from comprehension.

### Gaps
- No study found on humour and sharing or retention for educational short-form video specifically.

## 7. Distribution mechanics: how the Shorts and TikTok feeds test and expand content

### Takeaway
Official TikTok sources confirm a **personalized ranking model** that predicts each user's likely interaction from signals such as likes, shares, comments, follows, watch behaviour and finishing a video, with diversity rules. YouTube has publicly discussed the "**viewed vs. swiped away**" metric. The widely repeated "**small test batch, then expand in waves**" model is **reported or lore**. I found no official document describing fixed test cohorts or thresholds.

### Cited Findings
- TikTok (June 2020, newsroom) says the For You feed ranks videos by user interactions (likes, shares, comments, follows, content created) and adjusts for content marked "not interested". (Its other listed factors, video information and device/account settings, are from prior knowledge and were not re-verified this session.) It avoids two consecutive videos with the same sound or creator, and skips duplicates and previously seen videos. New users get a generalized feed of popular videos. Grade: **strong as official description** (but generic, no weights) — [TikTok newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you/)
- On the TikTok Transparency Center, the system estimates the likelihood that a user interacts with each candidate video and serves the highest-scoring ones. Only moderated videos enter the pool. Grade: **strong (official)** — [TikTok transparency](https://www.tiktok.com/transparency/recommendation-system/?lang=hi)
- YouTube (Creator Insider Q&A, Aug 2023, Shorts product lead Todd Sherman, as reported) discussed **viewed vs. swiped away** as a key Shorts metric in the Reach tab and addressed creator algorithm questions. Exact weights and thresholds are not public. Grade: **moderate** (official remarks via press; primary video not retrieved) — [TechCrunch 2023](https://techcrunch.com/2023/08/25/youtube-demystifies-the-shorts-algorithm-views-and-answers-other-creator-questions/); [Search Engine Journal](https://searchenginejournal.com/youtube-explains-how-shorts-algorithm-works/494953); [Buffer summary](https://buffer.com/resources/youtube-shorts-algorithm/)
- The "tested with a small audience first; if retention is low or swipe-away is high, it stops being pushed" model, and numeric thresholds like "60%+ viewed", appear in vendor guides without primary sources. Grade: **anecdote / lore** — [vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-shorts-algorithm/); [Fliki](https://fliki.ai/blog/youtube-shorts-algorithm)
- Conflict: some 2026 vendor posts cite "Todd Beaupre" Creator Insider remarks and 2026 updates that I could not verify. Treat them as unconfirmed. — [Fliki](https://fliki.ai/blog/youtube-shorts-algorithm)

### Inferences
- The officially confirmed signals (viewed vs. swiped, watching to the end, shares and comments as interaction signals) all point to the **first 1-2 seconds** (the swipe decision) and **completion** as the levers a 30-45 s Short controls.

### Gaps
- There is no official YouTube document on the Shorts test-and-expand mechanics, and support.google.com did not surface in searches.
- The TikTok newsroom page text was summarized from search, not fully fetched. Its mention of "whether a user finishes a video" as a strong signal is from prior knowledge and was not re-verified this session.

## 8. History and edutainment specifically: "HistoryTok"

### Takeaway
Academic work on HistoryTok is **young and qualitative**: case studies, content analyses, digital ethnography. It highlights **present-past analogies**, POV/role-play formats and polarized memory content. There are accuracy concerns. I found **no large-scale performance analysis** of which history Shorts do best.

### Cited Findings
- A chapter (Erasmus University Rotterdam) on #historytiktok, #historymemes and #historylesson shows TikTok's affordances encourage **analogies between present and past**. Grade: **moderate** (qualitative) — [EUR Pure](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/historical-analogies-and-historical-consciousness-user-generated-/)
- A De Gruyter edited volume (2025) is described as the first extensive treatment of history on Instagram and TikTok. Grade: n/a (scholarly overview) — [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9783111360874/html)
- A 2026 arXiv preprint on AI-generated history "POV" TikToks notes that the TikTok Research API is restricted, so most HistoryTok studies rely on digital ethnography. Grade: **weak-moderate** (preprint) — [arXiv 2606.23300](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.23300)
- A content analysis of Spanish historical memory on TikTok found clear, majority **polarization** around the sides in historical conflicts. Grade: **moderate** (content analysis) — [Dialnet PDF](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/8507286.pdf)
- A German Federal Agency for Civic Education (bpb) practitioner overview covers history on TikTok and Instagram. Grade: **anecdote / practitioner** — [bpb.de](https://www.bpb.de/lernen/digitale-bildung/werkstatt/521549/history-auf-tiktok-und-instagram/)
- Commentary cites >135 billion #history views on TikTok, and a 2022 survey says one in four US TikTok users use it for education, with history among the top subjects. Grade: **weak** (survey source not traced) — [The Walrus](https://thewalrus.ca/?p=162376)
- Classroom study (Philippines, n=33): student-made TikTok mini-lectures improved history knowledge over 5 weeks. Grade: **weak** (small, no control noted) — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/2f29532b54334d1688fa49abceab5a7c)

### Inferences
- Formats with scholarly attention (POV and role-play, present-day analogy such as "the Vasa was the Titanic of the 1600s", and "how did they get this so wrong" framing) suggest what is salient on HistoryTok. None are performance-validated.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study of which history Shorts or TikToks perform best (topics, structure, length).
- No data on animated versus talking-head history content.
- Creator-specific analyses of popular channels did not surface.
