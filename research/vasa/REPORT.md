# Win the swipe, then tell one story

The evidence supports a single plan for the Vasa Short: **win the first one or two seconds with the failure itself (a grand warship tipping over), then tell one causal story at a brisk pace and pay off the opening promise before the end, in roughly 45-60 s**. The format the owner wants to work in is real and currently performing very well. "Ricks Clips" (@RicksClips3000) is a Shorts-only channel with **208K subscribers and 486M views**. Its current animated run started around **6 Oct 2026**, posts about **5 Shorts a week**, and its Shorts have a **median length of about 60 s**. Its best performers carry 5-7-word declarative titles about a curious object or a "why" ([YouTube About panel](https://www.youtube.com/@ricksclips3000/about)). YouTube's own statements say **what decides a Short's reach is whether viewers watch or swipe away, and then how long they stay**. YouTube says there is no ideal length, and that posting time and upload volume are not levers ([blog.youtube, Rene's Top Five](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/renes-top-five-march-4-2024/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/08/25/youtube-demystifies-the-shorts-algorithm-views-and-answers-other-creator-questions/)). The measured research on sharing and memory comes almost entirely from text, news and lectures, not vertical video. It points the same way: stories are recalled better than fact lists, high-arousal framing (awe, incredulity) beats grief alone, and narration should be brisk but not compressed. The Vasa story fits this well because the museum's own figures already make the hook: **about 1,300 m and about 20 minutes into its maiden voyage, it sank in light wind**. One limit applies to everything in the Vasa section: **none of the Vasa facts were re-checked against full museum pages**. They rest on search excerpts of vasamuseet.se. Several popular "facts", including the King forcing a second gun deck, crowds of diplomats watching, and Fleming's quote, must be softened or dropped.

## YouTube's official line: the swipe is the impression, and length is the story's call

The official statements boil down to one mechanism. Rene Ritchie, YouTube's creator liaison, relayed Shorts head Todd Sherman's account in a blog.youtube post. In the Shorts feed there are no thumbnails, so "you have to start playing the actual video (views) and see if someone watches (retention) or leaves (swipes away)". The first few hundred or thousand views work like thumbnail impressions in long-form. A Short that underperforms its feed neighbours "won't go much beyond those initial, impression-like views (though it may blow up another day)" ([blog.youtube, Apr 2024](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/renes-top-five-march-4-2024/)). Sherman described the same **seed-audience** search in 2023 ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/08/25/youtube-demystifies-the-shorts-algorithm-views-and-answers-other-creator-questions/)). YouTube Studio now measures this as **"Stayed to watch"**, "the percentage of times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds of a Short". Average view duration and average percentage viewed are **computed only over viewers who stayed**, so a Short can show a high percentage viewed while many viewers swipe ([YouTube Help 12220281](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281?hl=en)). The general recommendations article lists watch behaviour, likes, shares, comments, surveys and "not interested" feedback as signals. It does **not** contain the Shorts-specific signal list that blogs attribute to it, and it publishes no weights ([YouTube Help 16533387](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16533387?hl=en)).

On length, the official position has moved, but it has never named a target. In 2023 Sherman said "there's not a certain length that benefits creators" and that creators should think about "how long they need to tell their story" (TechCrunch's paraphrase) ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/08/25/youtube-demystifies-the-shorts-algorithm-views-and-answers-other-creator-questions/)). In October 2024 YouTube raised the Shorts limit to **3 minutes** and promised to "improve recommendations for longer Shorts in the coming months". No post found reports that this work shipped ([blog.youtube](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/tall-updates-coming-to-shorts/)). One rule bears directly on the owner's 45-60 s range: **"Any Short that is over one minute in duration with an active copyright claim of any type, including manual claims, will be blocked globally"**, while Shorts under a minute are unchanged ([YouTube Help 15424877](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15424877?hl=en)). The 60 s ceiling is therefore a hard limit if any third-party music or sound is used.

The length evidence is genuinely mixed, and the owner's 45-60 s range is the defensible reading of it, not a proven optimum. Measured data splits by outcome. **Median views favour very short Shorts, while engagement and total views favour longer ones.** Recommender research on Kuaishou shows that completion rate is biased toward short videos, which platforms correct for ([arXiv 2206.06003](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.06003v1)), so a lower percentage viewed on a longer Short is not automatically a penalty. The opposing evidence deserves weight too. On TikTok, **duration is the strongest predictor of whether a video is watched to the end** ([MPIDR](https://www.demogr.mpg.de/en/publications_databases_6118/publications_1904/journal_articles/exploring_the_limits_of_predicting_user_watching_behavior_with_short_form_videos_on_tiktok_8988/)). That is why "never pad" matters more than any particular number. Four things support 45-60 s: YouTube says length should follow the story, the closest academic analogue ranks 30-60 s best, the reference channel has settled at about 55-65 s, and the Content ID rule caps the top end at 60 s. If the Vasa story runs at 48 s, it should ship at 48 s.

## Shares follow awe and incredulity, but none of the sharing science is on video

The sharing literature is solid in direction and weak on transfer, so it can set the tone but not the edit. Randomised Upworthy headline tests across **~105,000 variants and 370 million impressions** found that each extra negative word raised click-through by **2.3%** for an average-length headline. The verified per-emotion result reverses the earlier note: **sadness words raised clicks slightly, fear and joy lowered them, and anger had no effect**, all by less than 1% per standard deviation ([Robertson et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10202797/)). On sharing, social users were **1.91 times more likely to share negative news** across 579 million Facebook and Twitter posts ([Watson et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11405697/)). Each moral-emotional word raised diffusion about **20%** in political tweets ([Brady et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5514704/)). The best-known study in the area, Berger and Milkman's finding that high-arousal awe, anger and anxiety drive sharing while low-arousal sadness suppresses it, has a verified citation but **an unverified abstract**, because the publisher blocked access ([Wikipedia: Viral phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viral_phenomenon)). The out-group effect in Rathje et al. (**+67% odds of a share per out-group term**) is real but political, and it does not belong in a history Short ([Rathje et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8256037/)).

For the Vasa, this argues for **awe at the ship plus incredulity at the failure**: they tested it, it nearly tipped at the quay, they sailed anyway. Grief over the dead should not carry the piece alone. The deaths still belong in the story, stated plainly. Sadness is not poison for attention (Robertson), but the sharing case for grief rests on the unverified Berger and Milkman result.

On structure, the strongest evidence in the folder is a meta-analysis of **more than 33,000 participants**. It found **stories understood and recalled better than expository texts (g = .55; memory g = .72)**, although heterogeneity is very high and publication bias probably inflates the effect ([Mar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/)). Curiosity-gap theory holds that a small, closable gap in something the viewer half-knows produces the most curiosity ([Golman & Loewenstein](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/Information%20Gaps--Golman%20and%20Loewenstein.pdf)). A small study found clickbait **reduced the credibility** of the item ([White Rose](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/168209/1/Clickbaits_PURE.PDF)). The practical reading is to open a gap ("why did it sink?") and close it on screen.

Comments and rewatches have the thinnest evidence. Toxic and controversial comment threads correlate with engagement but cost monetisation ([arXiv 2408.00534](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00534v1)). **No study supports planting deliberate errors to bait corrections.** The legitimate route is a question historians actually argue about, and the Vasa offers one: the inquiry punished nobody, and blame fell on the dead designer ([Vasa Museum: The Inquest](https://www.vasamuseet.se/en/explore/vasa-history/inquest)). Classroom research finds that humour improves perceptions of the teacher and has mixed effects on learning. It works best when it relates to the content and is non-hostile (Banas et al. 2011; findings secondary only) ([Faculty Focus](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/effective-teaching-strategies/humor-in-the-classroom-40-years-of-research/)). Sailors running across the deck to test a ship that then nearly capsizes is exactly that kind of content-bound gag. History content specifically has only qualitative study. Present-past analogies stand out on #historytiktok ([EUR](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/historical-analogies-and-historical-consciousness-user-generated-/)), and **no quantitative study shows which history Shorts perform best**.

The route ran from Tre Kronor quay past Slussen and the Södermalm cliffs to the Tegelviken gap and Beckholmen ([The Disaster](https://www.vasamuseet.se/en/explore/vasa-history/disaster)). The look of Tre Kronor castle (a tall tower crowned with three crowns) and the 1628 flags are **UNVERIFIED**. They can be stylised, but the video should not claim accuracy for them ([Wikipedia: Tre Kronor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tre_Kronor_(castle))). Superlatives such as "the most powerful warship in the world" appear in none of these sources and should stay out of the title and narration.

## Twelve rules for the script and edit

Each rule cites its evidence. Grades: **Official** means a verified YouTube primary statement. **Strong**, **moderate** and **weak** follow the notes' scale. **Lore** means a convention with no traceable data. **[T!]** marks evidence that does not come from short vertical video.

## Conclusion

Very little of the advice behind viral Shorts is actually measured. YouTube's verified statements give only a mechanism: the swipe is the impression, and retention is the click. No study measures hook types, cut rates, caption formats or loops on Shorts. The rules above therefore carry real weight in only two places, the opening seconds and the story structure, plus one hard platform constraint, the 60 s Content ID ceiling. Everything else is a defensible convention borrowed from film, lectures or news. The practical consequence is to test, not trust. Each Vasa upload's Stayed-to-watch and percentage-viewed figures will say more about this channel's audience than any vendor benchmark. A hook variant can be compared only against the channel's own previous Shorts of similar length.

The Vasa's strength as a topic is that its most dramatic version is also its most accurate one. The museum's own numbers make the hook: about 20 minutes, about 1,300 m, a light breeze, open gunports, and a stability test that was stopped and then ignored. The myths that need cutting (the King's mid-build second deck, the storm, the quotable admiral) are not needed for the drama. Two checks are cheap to do before the script is locked and are worth doing: read the museum's Disaster and Inquest pages in full, and confirm the reference channel's older-video view total. Those checks address the two weakest foundations of this report, which are excerpt-based facts and one channel's still-unfolding breakout.
