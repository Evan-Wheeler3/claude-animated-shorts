# Why coins have ridges: fact sheet

Researched 2026-11-25. **Sources read:** Royal Mint Museum (RMM) pages fetched in full, and Wikipedia articles.
**Not reachable:** US Mint (403), Perth Mint, British Museum and Old Bailey search (no results came back). The Old
Bailey trial record was not read.

Use column: **Safe** = narrate as written. **Soften** = hedge or keep general. **Avoid** = leave out.

| # | Fact | Source | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | Before machines, coins were hammered by hand and came out irregular. "Coins were liable to suffer from clipping" | [Wikipedia: Hammered coinage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hammered_coinage) | Safe |
| F2 | Clipping is "shaving off a small portion of a precious metal coin for profit. The precious metal clippings are saved up and melted into bullion" | [Wikipedia: Debasement (Coin clipping)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debasement) | Safe |
| F3 | A silver coin's value was the silver in it (the reason clipping paid) | Implied by F2. The 1696 proclamation took hammered silver "at five shillings and Eight Pence an Ounce", i.e. by weight ([Wikipedia: Great Recoinage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recoinage_of_1696)) | Safe as "worth the silver inside it" |
| F4 | Clipping was treated like counterfeiting and "occasionally punished by death"; counterfeiting was high treason | [Wikipedia: Debasement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debasement); Newton article | Safe as "you could be put to death for it" |
| F5 | English minting was "finally mechanised in the early 1660s, shortly after the Restoration of Charles II" | [RMM: Making money](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/history/making-money/) | Safe |
| F6 | "In 1662 Charles II recalled Blondeau to establish a permanent machine-made coinage… a secret process for placing lettering or other designs on the edges… DECVS ET TVTAMEN, meaning an ornament and a safeguard, refers to the protection against clipping" | [Wikipedia: Milled coinage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milled_coinage) | Safe ("sixteen sixty-two"; "a pattern on the edge") |
| F7 | Machine coins after 1662 "were protected from clipping by an engraved, decorated and milled edge, but were instead forged" | [Wikipedia: William Chaloner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Chaloner) | Safe |
| F8 | "The inferior hammered coins limited the circulation of his coins until the hammered coins were demonetized" (1695/96) | [Wikipedia: Milled coinage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milled_coinage) | Safe ("the old smooth coins were still everywhere") |
| F9 | Great Recoinage, 1696: the government tried to "replace the hammered silver that made up most of the coinage in circulation, much of it being clipped and badly worn" | [Wikipedia: Great Recoinage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Recoinage_of_1696); RMM Newton: "recoinage of old silver coins dating back to the reign of Elizabeth I" | Safe |
| F10 | Newton became Warden of the Mint in 1696. After the recoinage his "main duty" was "investigating and bringing to justice those who clipped and counterfeited the coin of the realm" | [RMM: Isaac Newton](https://www.royalmintmuseum.org.uk/journal/people/isaac-newton/) | Safe |
| F11 | Newton "conducted interviews with criminals and informers" himself | RMM: Isaac Newton | Safe |
| F12 | Newton in disguise in taverns; 100+ cross-examinations; 28 coiners prosecuted | Wikipedia Isaac Newton article, read only as a summary | **Avoid** (not verified verbatim) |
| F13 | **Newton did not invent ridged edges.** RMM: "he was not an innovator". Edge marking dates from 1662 (F6). Wikipedia's Reeding page credits Newton (1698, citing the Perth Mint); RMM contradicts it | RMM: Isaac Newton; F6 | **Avoid** "Newton invented ridges" |
| F14 | William Chaloner was a serial counterfeiter "taught the subtle techniques of moulding 'milled edges'". Newton pursued him and "fielded eight witnesses" at the Old Bailey in March 1699. He was hanged at Tyburn | [Wikipedia: William Chaloner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Chaloner) | Safe. The date is disputed (22 vs 16 March), so give no day |
| F15 | By 1696 about 10% of the currency was forged | Wikipedia: Chaloner, Great Recoinage | Soften. Not narrated |
| F16 | Reeding today "helps people with visual impairment to identify different coin denominations by means of touch alone"; US quarters and dimes are reeded | [Wikipedia: Reeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reeding) | Soften ("help") |
| F17 | Modern milling may be "a deterrent to counterfeiting, an aid to the blind… or purely decorative" | Wikipedia: Debasement | Safe as "partly tradition" |
| F18 | US quarters and dimes have not been silver since 1965 | **UNVERIFIED** (US Mint returned 403) | Narrated only as "today's coins are not silver" |
